_This week we had guests from abroad visiting our group at the University of Osaka. What began as small talk over lunch turned into a long conversation about artificial intelligence and what it means for science, for teaching and for the people who do both. I have reconstructed it here from memory. The quotations convey the gist of what was said, not anyone's exact words._

---

### A tool we already live with

We started from the point nobody at the table disputed: AI is already part of our lives, and a genuinely useful part. Most academics I know use it every day, for very different purposes. My own use is modest. I let it check the grammar of my emails, and then I simply stop worrying about that part.

But usefulness was only half of the picture. The other half was unease. AI is improving at a pace that human beings, individually and as institutions, are finding very hard to match. Almost everything we said over the next hour or so was, in one way or another, about that gap.

### Smart, but not wise

I began with my own experience. So far, I have never felt that AI produces genuinely new ideas.

It is excellent at searching the existing literature, finding data, and pointing out the things we missed while reading papers, and it summarizes them well. It has also become remarkably good at computing. Given access to Python or Mathematica, it can carry out calculations, sometimes more accurately than a person, and it is very helpful for writing code.

What it still seems to lack, at least for now, is the ability to produce a new idea. I tried to put it in a single sentence: _AI is smart, but not wise. It can interpolate, but it cannot extrapolate._

Extrapolation, stepping outside the region where we have data or precedent, is exactly where physics lives. A large part of what physicists actually do day to day, however, is interpolation: careful, technical, and in principle automatable. I said that this might not be such a bad thing.

### An optimistic aside: the end of "publish or perish"?

I even allowed myself some optimism. Academia has long suffered from a culture of _publish or perish_. To get a job, you need publications, and the pressure to cross that threshold pushes researchers to care more about quantity than quality. In my view, this has been dragging science down.

If machines can now produce routine papers in large numbers, then counting papers may finally lose its meaning as a measure of a scientist. I said I hoped the pressure would ease, and that originality and depth would matter more again.

### "You are being too optimistic"

At this point my colleagues objected, and their objections were serious.

The first was about access. For the first time in the history of science, they said, research will depend on tools that not everyone can use. The most powerful AI models are available to some people and not to others, and those who have them will do research with far stronger instruments than everyone else.

This cuts against something very deep in academic culture. Physics, unlike most industries, is built on openness. Papers are freely available. People are glad to answer questions, to explain what they did, what they learned, and what they could not do. Now we are starting to work with machines whose reasoning we sometimes cannot follow at all.

One of our guests put her fear plainly. Over time, she said, the human researcher might be reduced to a mere _digester_ of progress made by machines. And if the pace keeps accelerating, even digesting it may eventually be beyond us.

### Advice we can no longer give

I agreed, and added an observation that has been bothering me for some time.

For the first time in history, we cannot tell our students or our children what to study so that it will still be relevant in twenty years. Twenty or thirty years ago, a parent or a professor could give that kind of advice with reasonable confidence. Today we cannot say it about the next five years, and perhaps not even about the next six months.

That is a measure of how fast society, academia and the world are changing. When change outruns our ability to adapt, people risk becoming irrelevant, not because they are less capable than before, but because the ground keeps moving under them.

### Power, collaboration and freedom

The table agreed, and the conversation turned to power.

The most capable models are concentrated in the hands of a few companies. My colleagues worried that this will make international collaboration harder. Companies may not allow their scientists to extend the same tools and privileges to collaborators elsewhere, and that could break the culture of open collaboration that academia depends on.

There is a further shift. These companies increasingly decide which scientists get access and what those scientists work on. For more than a century, researchers have been broadly free to choose their problems and their collaborators, and universities have been open to very large numbers of people. That may no longer be true.

One guest's deepest worry was simple. What happens to science if these companies decide to close their doors and stop letting outsiders use their strongest models? The result, she said, could be a kind of dictatorship of AI companies. With that much knowledge and capability concentrated in a few hands, it could become harsh and dangerous, whether or not AI ever becomes conscious. The concentration of power we already see, in particular hands chosen by very few people, is enough to make this a real concern.

I could not have agreed more.

### Why we need more education, not less

Then we came to teaching. My colleagues feared that governments will conclude they need to train fewer and fewer people, and that funding for universities will be cut accordingly.

I argued the opposite: we will need _more_ people with advanced training, not fewer. Think about what counted as basic education a few generations ago. It meant learning the alphabet, reading and writing. Today, working with computers is no longer optional. It is a skill one simply must have. In a world shaped by AI, many more people will be needed to understand, check and interpret what AI produces, so society will need more educated people than ever before.

Whether governments will see it that way, I honestly do not know.

### What the tedious calculations were really for

This brought us to what the work of a physicist was ever about. The point of physics was never to do hard computations. The calculations that AI now handles so well were never the goal. They were the difficult road we had to travel to reach answers we cared about.

But that road had a hidden reward. While grinding through long, tedious calculations, we learned techniques, and we often stumbled on questions that later turned out to be more important than the one we started with. History is full of such accidents. Kepler spent years fighting with Tycho Brahe's planetary data before he gave up circles and found elliptical orbits. Planck was trying to fit a formula to blackbody measurements when he introduced the quantum, a step he himself regarded as a mathematical device.

This is why teaching has become so difficult. Students can now get the answer to almost any problem we set, and it is very hard to convince them that they still need to learn how to solve it. With AI at hand, they can solve problems without ever learning _how_ problems are solved.

The only approach I can see is to give students questions that are genuinely worth pursuing, and to show them a perspective: what they are learning, and why it matters. If they understand where the path leads, they may choose to walk it themselves rather than letting the machine walk it for them.

### The question that decides everything

I closed my side of the argument with the question I think everything comes down to.

We never did physics in order to perform hard calculations. We did it to answer questions, and in that sense we are now better equipped than ever. But suppose AI does become wise. Suppose it learns to extrapolate, produces new ideas, solves problems and hands us the answers. Will society still insist that humans must understand and digest what AI has found?

If we answer this correctly, and accept that people need to understand the progress made by AI, then human understanding becomes the bottleneck. That is a good thing. It sets the pace of progress at a speed humans can follow, and keeps us relevant.

If society answers wrongly, or answers correctly while the AI companies ignore the answer and push ahead simply because AI is practically useful, whether or not anyone understands it, then we may face very serious and very dangerous situations that we will not know how to handle.

### A revolution, and a responsibility

We ended where all of us, I think, had already arrived. A revolution is underway, perhaps one of the largest in human history, and it could produce chaos and dangerous outcomes.

Academics have a particular role to play here. We have watched what has happened recently in mathematics, and we understand better than most what these systems can and cannot yet do. We should come together and speak up about the path the AI companies are taking.

Technology has usually been good for us, and we have usually adapted to it. But every time before, we were in control of what we were building. This time I am not sure we are. We need to raise our voices, slow the pace down, and make sure that we do not lose control.
