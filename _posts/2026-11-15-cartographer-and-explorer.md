**_Abstract:_** _AI is changing one profession after another, and physics, and science more broadly, are next. In this essay I argue that today's AI is a superb cartographer but not yet an explorer, look at what this means for researchers, students, the control of scientific tools and society, and end with my main worry: that science may soon be produced faster than humans can understand it._

Every revolution has a moment when it stops happening to other people. With AI, that moment is moving through the professions one by one, and it is moving fast.

Programmers felt it first. Within a few years, much of the everyday work of writing code could be handed to a machine.

Mathematicians came next, and for them it arrived suddenly. On 8 September 2026, OpenAI announced that about 10,000 AI agents had worked for 88 hours and found a “blow-up” in the forced version of the Navier–Stokes problem, one of the Clay Millennium Prize Problems [<a href="#ref-1">1</a>, <a href="#ref-2">2</a>]. The result came wrapped in controversy. It rested heavily on a strategy developed by Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa, a competing human–AI team disputed the credit, and independent researchers questioned the formal proof [<a href="#ref-2">2</a>, <a href="#ref-3">3</a>]. The unforced problem is still open [<a href="#ref-4">4</a>].

Less than a month later, on 6 October, OpenAI published more than 700 AI-generated manuscripts in a single day: 722 manuscripts in 372 families of results, claiming solutions to hundreds of open problems [<a href="#ref-5">5</a>, <a href="#ref-6">6</a>]. They were released on GitHub, without author names and without failure rates, and three had to be withdrawn within days because of an error [<a href="#ref-6">6</a>, <a href="#ref-7">7</a>]. I find the manner of this release deeply troubling. Results that whole communities had worked towards for years were dropped on them overnight, with no one to present them, explain them, or answer questions. The Association for Human Mathematics called the release “a demonstration of power” rather than scholarship and asked mathematicians to stop cooperating with the company [<a href="#ref-8">8</a>]. A Fields medallist said he was “paralysed”. Terence Tao saw clever new ideas in the proofs, but was frustrated that there was no one to discuss, present and teach the work [<a href="#ref-7">7</a>].

Now it is our turn, as physicists. At the end of October, OpenAI claimed a proof that four-dimensional Yang–Mills theory, the mathematical backbone of the Standard Model, exists, without releasing the 1147-page proof [<a href="#ref-9">9</a>]. And sooner or later, the rest of society will feel the same thing.

I want to write about what this looks like from my perspective as an academic, because I believe we are seeing early what many others will see later.

## Where AI stands: a cartographer, not yet an explorer

Artificial intelligence has become an indispensable part of our lives. In academia it has already changed the way we work. Researchers use it to search the literature, summarize papers, connect results from different sources, write code and analyze data. Given access to Python or Mathematica, it can carry out numerical computations, manipulate equations and reproduce complicated calculations, and it can even help write the paper. Tasks that once took hours or days can now sometimes be done in minutes.

I use these tools myself and find them genuinely useful. For me, the biggest benefit is still their remarkable writing ability: even something as simple as checking the grammar of an email saves time and removes an unnecessary source of frustration. The editing of this article was done with the help of Claude. In research I am more careful. AI helps me review the literature more thoroughly, find papers I might have missed, sometimes double-check my calculations, and point me to papers where similar technical calculations have already been done. But every time I hand a task to AI, I stop practicing a skill that is useful in my job. So I try to ask myself how important that skill is.

Despite all these abilities, I feel that something fundamental is still missing. AI has absorbed an enormous part of humanity's knowledge, and much of its accumulated wisdom too. It can process vast amounts of information, recognize patterns and work skilfully with what is already known. But can it genuinely go beyond what is known? Can it produce the kind of original insight that changes how we understand the world?

The simplest way I can say it: **AI is a superb cartographer, but not yet an explorer. It can interpolate, but it cannot yet extrapolate.**

A cartographer can draw a remarkably detailed map of everything that has already been charted: every road, every coastline, every known path, in more detail than any single traveler could remember. But the map stops at the edge of what is known. An explorer is the one who walks off the edge of the map, into territory nobody has described, and comes back with something new.

Everything I listed above happens inside the map. It is interpolation, and AI is already very good at it.

These are important skills, and most scientific progress depends on working with existing knowledge. But the most important moments in science usually come from stepping outside what we already understand.

Take theoretical physics. A physicist may spend weeks or months solving equations, checking numerical results and comparing approximations. Much of this work is technical and repetitive. But the point of these calculations is not just to get a number. It is to understand something we did not understand before. A new theory, an unnoticed mechanism or an unexpected link between two phenomena does not come simply from calculating faster. It starts with finding a question worth asking.

Of course, AI can suggest hypotheses and combine ideas in ways that have never appeared in the literature, and it is getting better at it. The harder question is whether it can tell on its own which unexplored directions are physically meaningful, which assumptions deserve to be challenged, and which seemingly unrelated observations point to a new principle. Producing something new is not the same as understanding why it matters.

For now, AI is much better at moving through the territory we have already mapped than at deciding where we should go next.

## The hopeful reading: an end to “publish or perish”?

There is one consequence of this that I find genuinely hopeful.

For years, academic research has suffered from the culture of _publish or perish_. Researchers are judged by their publication records, citation counts and other numbers. These measures are convenient for institutions, but they do not necessarily reflect the intellectual value of someone's work.

The results are familiar. Young researchers are under enormous pressure to publish often, sometimes at the cost of pursuing hard questions that might take years to answer. Small, incremental results become more attractive than ambitious research, because they are easier to deliver before the next deadline. So scientists are officially asked to do original research, while the system rewards the number of papers more reliably than their originality.

AI might break this. If machines can do the routine calculations, run the numerical analyses, check existing results, and in short automate most of what goes into an incremental paper, even writing the final version ready for submission, then simply being able to publish will slowly lose its value as a sign of scientific achievement.

That would be a chance to rethink what we want from researchers. Instead of rewarding someone for writing ten papers, we could ask whether they found an important problem, brought a genuinely new perspective, or changed our understanding of a phenomenon. I would welcome that change. It would let scientists spend less time on incremental results and more time on fundamental questions.

But this hopeful scenario assumes that our institutions respond wisely, and there is no guarantee they will. AI could just as easily flood us with low-value papers and make the publication race even worse.

The technology itself does not decide which way we go. Our institutions do. And that is where my concerns begin.

## The researcher: the hidden value of doing things the hard way

I am not convinced that automating scientific calculations will automatically make us better scientists.

The slow and often tedious process of research has always had a value beyond the result itself: it is often along the way that we find things we were not looking for.

History offers many examples. Johannes Kepler spent years analyzing Tycho Brahe's astronomical observations. He started out trying to understand planetary motion within the ideas of his time, but he could not make the data fit circular orbits. That difficulty eventually forced him to give up the traditional picture and discover that planets move on ellipses.

Max Planck was trying to understand the spectrum of blackbody radiation. In his attempt to reproduce the measurements, he introduced the idea that energy comes in discrete quanta. That idea became one of the foundations of modern physics and reached far beyond the problem he was originally trying to solve.

These discoveries did not come from applying known techniques to known problems. They came from long engagement with a problem, including the failures, surprises and conceptual difficulties along the way.

This is exactly what worries me about automation. Suppose a researcher asks AI to do a complicated calculation and gets the correct answer immediately. They save hours, perhaps weeks, and that is clearly a benefit. But what if, during those weeks of working by hand, they would have noticed an unexpected cancellation, questioned an approximation, or found a mathematical structure that suggested a completely different physical picture? The calculation was not just an obstacle between the researcher and the answer. It was also a chance to discover something.

I am not arguing that we should keep tedious calculations just because earlier generations had to do them. That would be unreasonable. But we should recognize that automating a task can also remove chances for learning and discovery that used to come with doing it. The challenge is to keep those chances while still making use of the technology.

## The student: what education is for

The same problem is even sharper in education, and it may be the most immediate challenge AI brings.

For centuries, education has been about acquiring knowledge and developing intellectual skills. We teach mathematics so students can solve problems, physics so they can understand nature, and programming so they can turn ideas into working procedures. But what happens when a machine can do many of these things faster, and sometimes more accurately, than the student?

A student can now ask AI to solve a differential equation, derive an expression, write a numerical program, or explain the solution to almost any textbook problem. The line between knowing _how_ to solve a problem and knowing how to _get_ its solution is disappearing. So students may well ask: why should we spend years learning techniques that a machine can carry out in seconds?

This question arises only because it may not be obvious to students that the aim of solving a problem was never the solution itself. Solutions to textbook problems have always been easy to find, in the back of the book or from a classmate. We ask students to solve problems because working through them is how they learn to think. What AI changes is how easy it has become to skip that work.

Take a physics student learning quantum mechanics. The solutions of the Schrödinger equation for the standard textbook potentials are already available. What matters is what the student learns on the way: how to set up a physical problem, recognize the relevant assumptions, choose the right mathematical methods, handle boundary conditions, and understand what a solution actually means. If AI hands over the final expression immediately, the student gets the right answer and misses the whole point of the exercise.

The same is true for programming. A student who asks AI to write a simulation may end up with a working program without understanding numerical stability, discretization errors, or the physical approximations behind it. They have a result, but not the knowledge needed to judge whether to trust it.

This is not a small problem. It goes to the heart of what education is for.

I am not an expert in education, and I do not claim to be. My aim is simply to raise the question. Nonetheless, from my own experience, I would suggest that we change the way we motivate students. Instead of only asking them to solve problems, we should help them see why a problem is interesting in the first place. We should teach them to recognize important questions, appreciate the physical meaning of mathematical structures, and develop the curiosity to explore things that do not yet have answers. A student who understands why a problem matters may still want to learn how to solve it, even when a machine could give them the solution.

## The field: who controls the tools of science?

During my visit to Kavli IPMU at the University of Tokyo, Tom Melia organised a brainstorming session attended by great minds such as Tsutomu Yanagida, Belén Gavela and many others. This section is my understanding of that discussion, and of a conversation I later had with Belén Gavela and Enrique Álvarez (IFT UAM/CSIC). The credit for these ideas belongs to them; any shortcomings in how I present them are mine. There, we came across the problem that worries me most of all. It is not about a single researcher or a single student. It is about who will own science itself.

Science has traditionally been built around a fairly open international community. Researchers publish their findings, share their methods, discuss their failures and work with colleagues across institutions and borders. Results are checked in public, by anyone who has the training to check them. This openness is not perfect, but it is one of the defining features of modern science, and much of the trust we place in science comes from it.

AI changes this, because the most powerful tools are concentrated in the hands of a few private companies. Their best models are not public. They are trained on data that no one outside can inspect, with computing power that no university can afford, and they run behind closed doors. The Navier–Stokes result and the October release in mathematics both came from internal models that no outside researcher could use or examine [<a href="#ref-1">1</a>, <a href="#ref-5">5</a>]. The Yang–Mills claim came without its proof [<a href="#ref-9">9</a>]. We were shown the results, but not the instrument that produced them.

Access to these tools is also unequal. Some researchers and institutions can use very capable systems, while others must rely on weaker alternatives. Most disturbingly, it is the private company that decides who gets such a privilege. Imagine two groups working on the same problem. One has an advanced AI system that can search the literature, do complicated calculations, analyze large datasets and explore possible directions. The other has only conventional computing resources. Even with equally talented people, the two groups are no longer on equal footing. As these tools become more important for research, access to them may decide who can do competitive science at all. That would be a new kind of inequality: between countries, between institutions, and between rich and poor universities.

There is also a problem for international collaboration. If private companies control access to advanced models, researchers may not be free to share the same tools with their collaborators. A scientific project could come to depend on whether every member has access to the same systems, and on the terms a company is willing to offer them.

More fundamentally, private companies may come to decide not only _how_ science is done, but _which_ science gets done. A company decides which problems its models attack, which results are released and when, and on what terms the rest of us may use its tools. Those choices follow commercial and strategic interests, not the questions a scientific community would choose for itself. The events of this autumn showed it clearly: the timing was set by a company racing a competitor, and the mathematical community learned about results in its own field after the fact. For more than a century, researchers have been largely free to choose their questions and their collaborators, and universities have been a place to study subjects with no immediate commercial value. That freedom is not guaranteed in the world we are entering.

Let me say this directly: the agenda of universities and traditional academia is very different from the agenda of private AI companies. What drives a company is financial gain, and there, money speaks. A university exists, at least in principle, to understand the world and to pass that understanding on to the next generation, whether or not it ever pays. I do not want to pretend that academia is free of money. Funding, grants and prestige shape our choices too, more than we like to admit. But the contrast between the two agendas is still enormous, and it is very concerning that the tools which may soon decide the future of science are being built by organizations whose first question is not "what is true?" or "what is worth knowing?", but "what will be profitable?"

Followed to its end, this road leads to a world I find frightening: a world of new empires, built not on territory but on intelligence, and governed by AI companies. In such a world, the most advanced science would be done in sealed cells inside a few companies, out of the reach of the public and out of the reach of most scientists. The rest of us would receive its results the way a customer receives a product, without the means to check how it was made, to reproduce it, or to ask a different question. Universities, which for centuries have been the public home of knowledge, would become customers too.

We do not need to imagine conscious machines, or an AI that seeks power, to see this danger. The concentration of scientific capability in a few private organizations is already happening. In that sense, the question of AI is also a question of power.

In that session, almost everyone agreed on one thing: the academic community all over the world should develop its own public, open-source AI, collectively, across countries and institutions, in the same spirit in which physicists built CERN together because no single country could. Even if such a tool cannot compete with the giants of the industry, it would give researchers reliable and free tools of their own: tools that any scientist can inspect, that no company can switch off, and that keep at least part of the frontier of discovery in public hands.

## Society: we may no longer know what to tell the next generation

There is one more consequence that I find particularly unsettling, and it reaches far beyond academia.

For generations, parents and teachers could give young people fairly simple advice: study mathematics, learn engineering, become a physicist, master a profession. No career was guaranteed, but it was reasonable to expect that what you learned would stay useful for decades.

Today I am not sure we can give that advice with the same confidence. We do not know which professions will remain unchanged over the next twenty years. More surprisingly, we may not even know which technical skills will still be valuable in five. What programmers experienced first, and mathematicians after them, scientists are experiencing now, and everyone else will experience soon. The pace of change is hard to predict, and the institutions responsible for education and training are struggling to keep up.

This raises a serious question about the future of universities. Some governments may conclude that if AI can do more and more intellectual work, society will need fewer highly educated people. Why train thousands of engineers, scientists or analysts if machines can do much of their work?

I believe this would be a fundamental mistake. A few generations ago, basic education mainly meant learning to read, write and do simple arithmetic. As society became more technological, knowing how to use computers became essential. Today it is hard to take full part in society without that knowledge.

The same will happen with AI. As machines become more capable, _more_ people will need the training to understand, supervise, evaluate and interpret what they produce. The ability to question a result, spot a wrong assumption, and tell a meaningful conclusion from a convincing but misleading one will matter more and more. AI should not make education less important. It should make it more important, and more widely available.

The danger is that governments and institutions reach the opposite conclusion, especially if they focus on short-term economic efficiency rather than the long-term intellectual needs of society.

## The central question: must humans understand?

All of this leads to the question I find most important: in the future, will humans still need to understand science, or only to use it?

Let me return to the cartographer and the explorer. Today's AI can draw and extend the map faster and more completely than any of us. What is much less clear is whether it can walk off its edge: extrapolate into the genuinely unknown, ask the question nobody has asked, or decide which direction is worth walking in.

It is possible, and some would say likely, that this will change. Imagine an AI that proposes a new physical theory, derives its predictions, and checks them against the data, all correctly, but in a form no human can follow. Would we accept it? Would we build on it? Would we still call it _our_ science?

Some will argue that human understanding is simply a bottleneck: if a machine can find the right answer, why should it matter whether we understand it? I disagree. Science has never been only a list of correct answers. It is the way humanity makes sense of the world. Understanding is what lets us judge a result, see where it may fail, notice when it is being used badly, and decide what to do next. A civilisation that holds correct answers it cannot understand is not more knowledgeable. It is more dependent.

## A revolution we must learn to govern

We are living through a revolution. Like every revolution, it will bring things we cannot yet imagine, good and bad. I use these tools myself, and I know how much they can give. But I cannot end this essay with reassurance, because I am not sure that reassurance is deserved.

Let me list what worries me:

- that by automating the slow work of research, we lose the surprises along the way that have so often led to real discoveries;
- that students get answers without learning how to think, and without the knowledge needed to judge whether to trust those answers;
- that governments and universities respond by cutting education, at the very moment we need more of it;
- that we no longer know what to tell the next generation to learn;
- that the most powerful instruments of science end up owned by a few companies, new empires in which science is done in closed cells, out of the reach of the public;
- and above all, that humans lose the ability to understand the science that is being produced.

This last point deserves the most attention. There is a threshold that we have not yet crossed, but are approaching fast: the point at which AI produces science and technology faster, and in a more complex form, than humans can follow. We can already see the first signs. The mathematical community is still trying to read and digest hundreds of manuscripts that were released in a single day [<a href="#ref-5">5</a>, <a href="#ref-7">7</a>], and checking even one of them carefully can take an expert weeks. Results are now being produced faster than people can understand them. If the same happens in physics, chemistry, biology and engineering, and at the current pace I believe it will, human scientists will slowly become irrelevant. Not because anyone forbids them to take part, but because they can no longer keep up. And a humanity that cannot understand the science and technology it depends on has already lost control of them.

This is why we need to discuss this threshold now, before AI crosses it, and not after. Once AI is producing knowledge that no human can understand, it will be too late to ask whether that is what we wanted.

What would it take? First, guardrails. A technology this powerful does not arrive with its own rules; we have to write them, as we did for medicine and for nuclear energy. Results produced with AI should be clear about how they were produced. Researchers should stay responsible for what they publish. Communities should be consulted, not surprised, when a company decides to release hundreds of results into their field overnight. And no result should be accepted as part of science until humans are able to check it and understand it.

Second, pace. The progress of AI should be slowed down to a pace that matches the pace of humans: a pace at which we can read, check, teach and absorb what is produced, and at which our institutions can adapt. Speed is not progress if no one understands where we are going.

Third, public tools. Academia all over the world should build its own AI together, so that the instruments of discovery do not belong only to a few companies. And fourth, education: if machines take over more of the routine intellectual work, we need more people, not fewer, who can understand, question and guide what those machines do.

I am not optimistic that any of this will happen in time. The incentives point the other way. Companies race each other, governments race each other, and the pace is set by whoever moves fastest. The most important question may not be whether AI will one day become smarter than us. It may be whether we will remain wise enough to decide what to do with it. At the moment, I am not sure that we are.
